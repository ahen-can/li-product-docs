^Hedging through Equity Derivative
The fund may use equity derivatives solely to hedge existing equity exposure, subject to
the applicable IRDAI framework and the Board-approved policy. The effectiveness of the
hedge will depend on market conditions and the movement of the underlying equity
portfolio.

                                            17
Hedging is intended to reduce the impact of adverse market movements. It may not fully
protect the fund because the investment and the derivative may move differently.
Hedging also involves costs and may reduce gains when markets rise.
Illustration 1: Hedging a Stock with a Put Option
Assume the fund holds 1,000 shares of Stock B at `100 per share, with a total value of
`1,00,000
To reduce the impact of a fall in the share price, the fund buys put options covering all
1,000 shares:
    • Strike price: `100
    • Premium: `5 per share
    • Total premium: `5,000
The following outcomes are simplified and assumed to occur at option expiry:
If the share price rises by 15% to `115: The shares increase in value by `15,000. The put
option expires without value. After deducting the `5,000 premium, the net outcome is
positive `10,000.
If the share price falls by 15% to `85: The shares decrease in value by `15,000. The put
option value is `15,000, resulting in net gain of `10,000. By buying put option, the
Company reduces its net negative outcome from `15,000 to `5,000.
If the share price remains at `100: There is no change in the share value. The put option
expires without value, resulting in net outcome of negative `5,000, equal to the premium
paid.
This example does not mean that every put-option hedge will limit losses to the premium.
The outcome depends on factors such as the strike price, quantity hedged, expiry, market
price, liquidity, transaction costs and taxes.
Illustration 2: Hedging a Portfolio with Index Futures
Assume the fund has an equity portfolio value is `1 crore, the current index level is 20,000
and each futures lot contains 140 units.
    • Value of one futures lot: `20,000 × 140 = `28,00,000
    • Futures sold: 1 lot
    • Unhedged portfolio: `1,00,00,000 − `28,00,000 = `72,00,000



                                            18
If the market and futures fall by 10%:
    • The share portfolio decreases by `10,00,000
    • The futures position provides an offset of `2,80,000
    • The portfolio therefore decreases by `7,20,000 (`10,00,000- `2,80,000) after
        hedging
By selling index futures, the Company reduces its loss from `10 lakh to `7.2 lakh when the
market falls by 10%. This example assumes that the portfolio and futures move by the
same percentage. In practice, their movements may differ, so the hedge may be more or
less effective than shown.
Potential Benefits of Hedging
    • May reduce the impact of adverse equity-market movements
    • May lower fluctuations in the portfolio value
    • May support disciplined management of equity risk

Key Risks - Hedging may involve basis risk, hedge ineffectiveness, opportunity cost,
liquidity risk, valuation risk, option-premium risk, margin requirements and transaction
costs. It may also reduce gains when markets move favourably.
Important Note - These simplified illustrations are provided only to explain how hedging may
operate under stated assumptions. They are not forecasts, promises or assurances of any
outcome. Actual results may differ materially due to market movements, hedge coverage,
pricing differences, liquidity, expiry, costs, taxes and other factors. Hedging does not guarantee
returns, eliminate losses or assure protection of capital.
